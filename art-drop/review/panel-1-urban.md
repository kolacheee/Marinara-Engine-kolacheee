# Panel 1 — urban plausibility

Reviewed all three Round 15 images at native size and in enlarged crops, all four house and six hall references in `reference/r15`, and the revised `BRIEF-17.md` in `marinara-art-r17-run`. `reference/r17/` exists but is empty. Locations below use the 1200 × 912 mockup, with coordinates measured from its top-left; north means image-top. The architectural model suggested by these references is a late-medieval/early-modern northern European timber town. It is not a template for every European town.

## 1. What works

Attached frontages, narrow premises, shops beneath dwellings, passage arches and unequal heights are useful ingredients. They establish more urban density than today's detached houses in lawns. Keep those ingredients. Today's house and hall sprites remain better references for buildings with volume: deep roofs, projecting wings, substantial foundations and entrances attached to a recognizable building type.

## 2. Problems, most important first

1. **There are rows, but no convincing urban blocks or properties.** Throughout the image, especially the middle-left row at x0–384/y400–672 and the bottom band below y750, buildings read as stacked front elevations. Roofs and coping-like strips overlap without explaining the land behind them. I cannot identify a long plot, a boundary running back from a shop, a rear workshop, or an enclosed working yard. The arch at approximately x550/y315 promises access to somewhere, but its destination is absent from the composition. The missing information is ownership and depth. Historic burgage plots could retain boundaries while acquiring successive rear ranges, yards and gardens; Bideford provides a documented example. [Devon historic environment record](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/heritage-gateway/results/record/c39bc47e-3b72-4a06-9863-681629e456a2/MDV103987).

2. **The centre has no institution capable of explaining the town.** The roughly 10 × 7-cell paved rectangle at x384–864/y336–672 contains scattered little stalls and a tiny well. There is no church, churchyard, guild entrance or market hall. Its north side is another commercial row; its other edges have no stronger identity. This is an empty forecourt with props. Neither a change of paving nor a taller ordinary house establishes a civic centre.

3. **The streets have almost equal importance, and buildings do not address them.** The 96-pixel strip at x864–960 and the broad east–west band around y672–784 consume substantial space without distinguishing a main route from service access. The middle-left and middle-right buildings still present their principal elevations toward the viewer, rather than turning toward the square or the north–south streets. Continuous paving exposes the stripe construction. Density needs narrower secondary routes, corners with doors, and destinations—not just taller rows.

4. **The wall is a backdrop rather than a connected defence.** Along y0–95, the battlement rhythm stretches across the entire visible frontage. Neither the street near x360 nor the stronger one near x910 meets a visible gate. There is no legible connection between a wall-walk, stair, tower and street. The render may be a crop of a larger circuit, but this visible segment does not demonstrate how the town gets through or uses its wall.

5. **Storey variation disguises weak building volume.** On the north-central row, x384–864/y35–336, long horizontal floor bands dominate the different heights. At 2× the windows and shop goods are elaborate; at 1× the floors become stripes. The shallow-looking roofs and missing side returns do not give those tall houses convincing bodies. Compared with `fantasy-house-2` and `fantasy-hall-3`, the additions look like extra slices of facade, rather than rooms under a deep roof. Heavy stone-looking upper courses also alternate with timber courses without a consistent structural or rebuilding story.

6. **Height and materials do not describe households, trades or districts.** The north-central and right-hand rows shuffle yellow, blue-grey, timber and masonry effects between floors. Nearly everything is a conspicuously decorated commercial house. There are height differences, but almost no low service ranges to explain them, no quieter residential frontage, and no coherent cluster of modest or wealthy premises. Five-storey houses are possible in the intended fantasy; making each narrow frontage equally assertive removes hierarchy.

7. **The ground and small objects have the wrong emphasis.** In the central square and the supplied detail image, individual flagstones rival a person's height in screen size, while stall canopies are only about 30–36 pixels wide. The ground reads more strongly than the market. Trees, benches, crates and people are scattered without showing a loading place, water queue, sheltered selling space or worn route. No visible gutter, threshold wear, wheel track or repaired patch connects daily use to the fabric. More random clutter would preserve the set-like effect.

## 3. Top six drawable changes

Sizes below are proposed game dimensions, not archaeological measurements. One cell is 48 pixels. Footprints and clear routes are ground-plan allocations; sprite height is specified separately. The streets and institutions will require replotting the mockup, not dropping large sprites onto its existing houses.

1. **Build one complete burgage block before making more facade variants. — C, with B**

   Replace the middle-left strip with four to six plots, each **1–2 cells wide and 5–7 cells deep**. Put a **2–3-cell-deep street house** at the front, **2–3 cells of yard** behind, and a **1-cell-deep shed or workshop** at selected plot ends. Draw dividing walls or fences perpendicular to the street, continuing past the rear wings. Give one wider property an L-shaped service wing and one narrow property a 24-pixel passage beside its house; the existing large arch should lead to a visible **2 × 2-cell court**. Connect the plot ends to a **1-cell service lane**.

   Draw a wood stack against a shed, washing in one yard, a small vegetable bed in another, and a privy at one rear boundary. Put a shared well in the accessible court, away from the privy. Use low foreground ranges and openings between wings so **at least two yards remain visible after roof occlusion**. A roof-filled block with no visible private ground fails this exercise too.

2. **Give the streets a hierarchy and make the buildings turn. — C; new streets/layout work, now E in the revised brief**

   Make the existing x864–960 corridor the **2-cell main route** from the north gate to the square. Retain that clear width through the market approach. Reduce an ordinary side street to **1 cell** and introduce a **half-cell pedestrian passage** connecting two yards or emerging through an arch. Replace part of the broad southern paving band with deeper plots; let its remaining street terminate at a guild entrance or turn around a churchyard boundary.

   Draw **two corner houses with 2–3-cell side returns**, an actual side door, and a shop window facing the north–south street. Introduce one **half-cell building-line projection** at an old plot boundary, with a low annex explaining it. Keep the route around it clear. Use small setts on the main street, packed earth and patch paving in service access, and a **4–6-pixel gutter** leading toward a visible discharge. Avoid identical raised kerbs everywhere. Let the street bend for a property or institution, not to satisfy a random-jog quota.

3. **Make a church precinct and a distinct guild property. — D, with C**

   Replace the eastern portion of the square's north frontage with an **east–west church footprint about 6 × 3 cells**: nave, narrower chancel at its east end, three readable buttresses, and a south porch opening toward the market. Add a **2 × 2-cell west tower**, with its top at least **48 pixels above the tallest neighbouring house roof**; budget roughly **9–10 cells of total sprite height** if necessary. A substantial parish church is sufficient. It need not automatically be a cathedral.

   Reserve a **6 × 2-cell churchyard strip** along its south side, enclosed by a low masonry wall with one **1-cell gate**, a worn porch path and a few modest grave markers. Let this enclosure indent the square. Put a **4 × 3-cell guildhall property** on the west edge, with an east-facing entrance, a broad upper meeting-room window, two ground-floor arcade bays, and a **2 × 2-cell rear service court**. Its bell turret can identify it without matching the church tower's height. These should read as two institutions with their own land and access, not matching ornaments flanking a plaza.

4. **Give the market a roof, usable trading space and a delivery route. — D**

   Enlarge the combined square and churchyard-edge allocation to about **12 × 9 cells**, moving the western and southern frontages outward to accommodate circulation. Place a market hall west of the square's centre, **2 cells east–west × 4 cells north–south**. Draw an open ground floor, **three structural bays**, stout posts or piers, visible knee braces where timber is used, and one deep continuous roof. Keep it to roughly **one tall storey plus roof**. A low hall can be a major landmark through footprint and silhouette; Chipping Campden's 1627 hall was built to shelter traders. [National Trust](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/gloucestershire-cotswolds/market-hall).

   Make six stalls **1 × 1 cell each**, arranged in two short groups beside the hall, with **a clear 1-cell customer aisle** and the **2-cell gate route** left unobstructed. Include one **1 × 2-cell cart/loading bay** at the hall's end. Set a cross or substantial well near the meeting of approaches, clear of that cart route. Use a few sacks, weighing equipment and baskets concentrated where they are handled. Replace the huge flagstone field with smaller paving and a worn delivery track. Trade should explain the arrangement.

5. **Connect the visible wall to the town through a working gate. — D; new circulation work/E**

   Centre a **3-cell-wide gatehouse** on the north end of the x864–960 route, re-spacing adjacent plots. Give it a **1-cell clear arch** between two flanking tower masses, a visibly deep passage about **2 cells long**, gate leaves, and a small toll hatch on the town side. Let the 2-cell road narrow at this controlled opening and widen into a **3 × 2-cell inner forecourt**, with room for one waiting cart.

   Continue the curtain wall into both towers at the same wall-walk level. Draw the wall-walk's top surface, an **internal stair one cell wide**, and one projecting interval tower toward the left of the visible wall. Keep a **1-cell service route** along its inner foot. Extend the north framing enough to show the road continuing outside; add a culvert where the street drain leaves. The gate must be recognizable as a passage through a thick boundary even without banners.

6. **Draw a believable sequence of households and alterations. — A and B; district placement in new/E**

   Make a sample of **eight adjacent frontages**: one five-storey merchant house, two three-storey houses, three two-storey houses, and two single-storey workshops or annexes. Concentrate the taller houses near the market; place the low ranges on side streets and behind them. For the merchant house use a stone ground floor, timber/plaster above, one **12-pixel jetty**, and a **24-pixel projecting oriel**. Give two ordinary houses plain domestic doors instead of retail counters.

   Use warm lime plaster, dark timber and red tile as the main local family; reserve extensive dressed stone for the church, guild building and selected wealthy bases. Put a restrained slate roof on one prominent property, reused tiles or shingles on service buildings, and only occasional thatch in a suitable peripheral cluster. Continue each house's material logic around its side and rear. Draw **one blocked window, one patched roof area, one rebuilt brick chimney, and one added lean-to** across the sample. Expose the taller neighbour's plain party wall above a low roof. Put soot at a working chimney and abrasion at a busy threshold. Those specific alterations imply time and use more effectively than making everything crooked or dirty.

## 4. Where I disagree with BRIEF-17

- **The prescribed lots are too shallow.** “1–2 cells wide and deep” contradicts the desired burgage fabric and even the proposed 2–3-cell roof depths. Separate frontage width, house depth and whole-property depth. Use the deeper plots above, with occasional subdivision and amalgamation.
- **Roof coverage is being treated as density.** B says a row hides its yard; the critic rejects bare ground where roofs are expected. Explicitly protect planned yards, courts, passages and gardens from that test. The failure to reject is unexplained leftover paving, not all visible ground within blocks.
- **Every landmark should not exceed a five-storey house.** A market hall needs shelter and access; a fountain needs a basin. Neither needs tower height. The stated 5 × 6, 4 × 4 and 4 × 3 sprite sizes also conflict with the height rule if they are total image bounds: five 48-pixel floors already occupy 240 pixels before the roof. Define ground footprints and vertical art extents separately. Reserve skyline dominance for the church tower and selected civic or defensive towers.
- **Mandatory irregularity will produce another formula.** A bend, wedge and width change in every district is picturesque noise unless tied to old boundaries, terrain or routes. Regular planned plots and market layouts are historically legitimate; Herefordshire's account explicitly describes them. Keep some sustained straight frontage, then show a specific later encroachment or institutional boundary. [Herefordshire Archaeology](https://htt.herefordshire.gov.uk/1381.aspx).
- **The district rules are too categorical.** Slate is not a universal wealth code, and poor households do not automatically occupy low thatched cottages. Allow poorer accommodation behind prosperous frontages and over workshops; allow wealthy timber houses. Choose a regional material supply before applying status variations. Do not sprinkle tannery or dyer props into a generic craft quarter without water, working space and a discharge route.
- **“Neighbours share eaves” should describe a weatherproof junction, not universal alignment.** Different heights need abutting roofs, party-wall coping and intelligible drainage. Unbroken horizontal eaves would repeat the present stripe problem. Likewise, one roof colour per block is a useful dominant-palette target, but an absolute ban on a repaired or replaced roof erases age.
- **“Overlays never cross a lot edge” conflicts with the architecture requested.** Jetties, oriels, signs, eaves, cast shadows and washing across a lane can project beyond the ground property boundary. Define allowed projections and preserved route clearance. Do not clip them to a facade rectangle.
- **The production order should validate land use earlier.** Before finishing A's full facade set, compose one rough deep block, gate approach and institutional edge with placeholder volumes. Then judge the facade and roof assets in that layout. Otherwise A–B can pass beautifully while C–D remain impossible to fit.

**One-line summary:** Draw plots, working yards, connected routes and institutions with their own land; taller decorated fronts alone will keep this town looking like a set.
