## 1. What works

The church on the western square edge, northern guildhall, market hall, six stalls and public fountain establish recognizable civic functions. The northwest court has a back stair, storage and washing: useful evidence of habitation. The wall circuit, approximate tower intervals and interior clearance are plausible at this scale. The gate and church survive greyscale. Street frontages have no ornamental lawns.

## 2. Blocking problems

1. **The plots are narrow graphics, but their ownership and use remain unreadable.** Where: the northern blocks, especially `(144,288)–(432,720)` and `(1056,288)–(1776,720)`; also the southern red-roof groups around `(576,1056)–(1104,1344)`. Long roof strips do not by themselves communicate burgage plots. I need to read a street-front house, its rear extension, an enclosed working yard, and access to that yard. Here roof planes, exposed stairs, little sheds and public paving meet without enough consistent boundaries to explain which household owns what. The northwest court is a start; most neighbouring properties have no equally clear domestic sequence. The aligned northern roof backs and scattered southern groups still suggest a composed display. The comparison makes this particularly obvious: R15 had stronger continuous street enclosure, despite its other faults.

2. **Side streets are mostly residual space between south-facing buildings.** Where: the narrow north–south slots at `x432–480` and `x1344–1392`, and especially the broad eastern junction around `(1344,624)–(1632,768)`. The main gate route has a useful pale surface and a bend. Beyond it, large areas of paving open around building groups without sufficient frontage to define their role. The eastern junction spreads to roughly three cells deep; nearby narrow slots have few convincing entrances addressing them. A side street should collect doors and activity along its length. A service lane should lead to identifiable backs and yards. Too much of this network depends on subtle paving borders to explain the distinction. It remains too open and weakly addressed to read as a dense old town.

3. **The square's institutions have weak relationships to the space they supposedly frame.** Where: church `(480,624)–(720,1056)`, guildhall `(864,276)–(1056,672)`, and the pocket enclosure at `(576,672)–(720,768)`. The guildhall addresses the square, but the church's principal entrance addresses the street to its south; its square-facing side lacks a comparable public threshold. A side-on church can work, but this one needs an entrance or precinct connection explaining that arrangement. The tiny bench-and-tree enclosure reads as a separate sitting pocket rather than a coherent churchyard. Meanwhile the guildhall's body loses authority beside the enormous ordinary roof strips and sign-bearing fronts. The square contains the required objects, but its civic edges still need to be designed as places people enter and use.

## 3. Ranked suggestions

1. **Rebuild one complete block as actual properties, then repeat its logic.** Start with the northwest block at `x144–432`. Retain 48–96 px frontages; assemble the principal houses with 96/144 px roof-depth pieces, distinct eaves and visible party-wall divisions. Give each visible rear property a 48 px yard strip, a fence return, and one 48×48 shed, woodpile or washing area. Connect those yards through a clearly bounded 24 px passage or an arch from the public street. Keep the existing stair court, but explicitly separate its private yard from its access passage. Show this sequence in the northeast and southern blocks too. Do not try to express plot depth solely by extending uninterrupted roof texture.

2. **Make the street hierarchy structural.** Preserve the 96 px gate-to-market route and its bend. At the eastern junction, reclaim one 48 px strip of surplus paving with a shallow workshop frontage or a fenced service court; retain the actual through route. Use `side-ground`, `side-storey` and side-roof pieces along the north–south slots so at least two 48 px bays have doors or working openings onto each side street. Keep secondary streets 48 px clear and service passages 24 px clear. Put a 48 px setback or jog at selected junctions, rather than letting broad unbounded paving provide all the irregularity. Preserve the separate 48 px inner wall lane.

3. **Give the church a square-facing threshold and a connected precinct.** Add a 48×48 porch to its eastern wall near `(672,912)`, with an unobstructed 48 px approach from the market. Rework the existing northern pocket into a 144×96 enclosure visibly connected to the church by boundary returns and a 48 px gate. Packed earth, a path and a few restrained markers would distinguish it from ordinary street paving; no frontage lawn is needed. Reduce the adjacent northern domestic roof masses by one 48 px course where possible so the guildhall's roof and bell tower establish a clearer civic hierarchy. Keep its arcade approach open.

4. **Make the defences look operated.** The circuit and gate are serviceable; they are not a separate blocker. On the northern wall, show one 48 px stair/access bay reaching the wall-walk, or a clearly readable tower doorway at walk level. At the southern gate add recessed gate leaves or a portcullis groove and a 48×48 guard/toll bay beside the passage, outside its clear width. Give interval towers less visual emphasis than the gate through restrained roof, shutter and repair variants. Preserve roughly eight-cell spacing and the maximum of two identical pieces in any run of five; do not add more towers simply to fill space.

5. **Put social differences into building form.** In the southern ochre-roof groups around `(144,1056)–(384,1344)` and `(1392,1104)–(1776,1344)`, replace two tall domestic stacks with one- or two-storey houses and 48×48 lean-tos. Use the recovered space for enclosed working yards. Keep taller stone-bottomed merchant houses at the market and principal road. Roof colour alone does not establish a poorer quarter.

The district still reads as a fortified display of building groups. Its next revision needs convincing relationships between buildings, property boundaries and public space more than additional decorative objects.
